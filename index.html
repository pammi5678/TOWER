My argument

In “Tower,” the forest keeps telling the speaker that all is well while her own reflex keeps saying lose lose lose, and that direct conflict is what the poem is about. The snake, which only pretends to be dead, gives her a safe situation to practice slowing down, so she learns to manage her fear of death instead of erasing it.

The poem in objective terms

“Tower” is a free-verse poem in a single unbroken stanza, spoken by a first-person speaker in the present tense. It is set on a forest path between dogwoods and a meadow, where she encounters a snake that is pretending to be dead. The imagery is mostly natural (dogwoods, ageratum, leaves, slopes), and Queen uses personification when the insects and wind “say” that all is well. Metaphor shows up in panic’s thousand fingers braided through / my insides—false roots. Repetition (lose lose lose), a dash that works as a caesura, and enjambment slow or speed the reading to match her reflex and her pause. The tone moves from anxious to observant, and the poem turns at In the forest, grief lives a new life / as devotion.

From my essay

In the poem, Tower by Khadijah Queen, the speaker walks a forest path with her panic and her reading list, and meets a snake that pretends to be dead. Queen, who holds a PhD in English from the University of Denver and has published six books of poetry, is one of the poets in Ada Limón’s anthology of nature poetry (“Khadijah Queen”).

At first she treats death as a loss and flinches from it. By the end she imagines stepping over the snake, having learned something she could not get from the tarot or the books she can barely finish.

She knows about death, but only from a distance

  • She never describes death itself, only what she has read about it. Books and a tarot reading, always at arm’s length.
  • In small doses is the phrase that stuck with me. Grief is treated like a substance she can only handle a little at a time.
  • Ocean math reads to me like she turns sadness into something she can calculate so it stays small.
  • The tarot says let go, change, but she admits right before that her mind jumps to lose lose lose automatically. Knowing the advice doesn’t stop the reflex.
  • So she has plenty of knowledge about death and no practice with it. That’s the gap the snake fills.

From my essay

The speaker’s habit of keeping grief at a distance, through books she can only take in small doses and a tarot reading she consults, shows that she knows about death in the abstract but has no practice responding to it. The speaker is simply naming what she has and hasn’t read.

Her phrasing suggests that she can only take one book in small doses, as if grief were a substance that might overwhelm her if she consumed too much at once. Her next worry, that she doesn’t want to drown in ocean math, pairs a vast, dangerous image (the ocean) with a cold, abstract one (math), which suggests that she uses analysis to keep sorrow at a manageable size.

The tarot works in a similar way. It tells her to let go and change, but the poem places this advice immediately after her admission that she thinks lose lose lose automatically when she sees death. Reading and consulting give her knowledge, but the knowledge alone doesn’t stop her reaction.

(Queen, 2024) (Wolfe and Wilder 2015)

The snake is a teacher because it isn’t really dead

  • It plays dead and pretends to be a branch, so it is dead and alive at once. That is the line between living and dying she’s afraid of, in a form she can stand in front of safely.
  • The speaker later calls it a scam. That tells me she has stopped taking the scene at face value.
  • A real corpse would just prove her reflex right. A performing snake makes her reflex look questionable.
  • Her first instinct is to sort it fast: safe or dangerous, real or fake. That fits automatically.
  • What the snake teaches is that slowing down changes what she sees.

From my essay

The snake in the poem only pretends to be dead, it blurs the line between living and dying that the speaker fears, which makes it a safer and more effective teacher than a real death would be. On the surface, the snake is simply lying still on the path.

The poem describes it like a performance. The snake plays dead and pretends to be a fallen branch, and the speaker later calls the act a scam. A real corpse would confirm her reflex to be accurate, that death means loss, but this snake is viewed to be dead and alive at once, so her automatic response might be irrational.

Her first instinct is to sort what she sees into safe or threatening, real or fake, and to react at once. Seeing it as a quick-verdict situation fits the automatically reflex. The snake then teaches her that slowing down changes what she sees.

(Queen, 2024) (Wolfe and Wilder 2015)

The forest and her reflex are in direct conflict

  • Everything around her says All is well and tells her to trust instinct’s music. Her own instinct is panic. I think the poem is built on that clash.
  • She walks to unravel the panic, so she’s trying to loosen it, not erase it.
  • Her careful moves (don’t / move too fast, switch directions) show she’s still afraid. If the fear were gone she wouldn’t need to be careful.
  • Then she pause[s] and turns back to watch what the snake chooses sans my alarm. The alarm is still there, she’s just setting it aside.
  • The fear isn’t gone. It just stops deciding what she does.

From my essay

The speaker’s careful movements in the second half show that her fear is managed, not removed. This part of the poem argues that this carefulness shows she’s still afraid, but she’s handling the fear differently. If her fear had simply disappeared, she wouldn’t need to be careful.

The line I think lose lose lose automatically. could mean she doesn’t decide. Her mind reaches lose before she has thought anything. And then the line shortly after, then pause—turn back to see what choice the snake makes sans my alarm. she stops (pause), turns back, and gives the snake time to act.

She is now watching for a choice instead of reacting. She also said the snake sans (meaning without) her alarm. So, the fear isn’t erased, because the alarm still exists to be set aside. What changed is that it no longer decides what she does.

(Queen, 2024) (Wolfe and Wilder 2015)

Giving up means loss and release

  • On the surface it sounds like the lose lose lose reflex, as if death wins.
  • The turn is grief lives a new life / as devotion. Grief changes form instead of disappearing, just like her fear.
  • The leaves play at color before surrendering, which echoes the snake that plays dead. The forest’s performances end in surrender, not collapse.
  • The ending stays open: If I don’t look down, or walk away. She is moving toward stepping over the snake, not finished doing it.
  • So giving up is release, and the forest (not the books or the tarot) is where she gets to practice it.

From my essay

The ending’s claim that living and dying both require giving up turns on a double meaning, loss and release, and the forest teaches the second meaning by modeling it. On the surface, giving up sounds like the lose lose lose reflex, as if death wins. But the forest has been offering another answer from the start.

The midges, dragonflies, moths, and wind all say All is well and urge her to trust instinct’s music, while her own instinct is panic, so the land and her reflex are in direct conflict. The poem then states the shift plainly: In the forest, grief lives a new life / as devotion. Grief is not removed but changes form, just as her fear is managed instead of erased.

The leaves model the same change, since they play at color before surrendering, an echo of the snake that plays dead, so the forest’s performances end in surrender instead of collapse. Even the final steps are hedged: she imagines stepping over the snake only if she doesn’t look down, or walk away, which suggests the change is something she is moving toward, not something finished. Giving up, then, is not defeat but release, and it is the forest, not the books or the tarot, that lets her practice it.

(Queen, 2024) (Wolfe and Wilder 2015)

Why this poem can’t be read just any way

Poems invite more than one reading, but the words still set limits. I can’t say the speaker is calm at the end, because her alarm, her panic, and her careful steps are all still there. I also can’t say she is crushed by death, because the poem has the forest call it all well and has grief turn into devotion. And the ending doesn’t let me say she has fully changed, since stepping over the snake sits inside an if. What I can say is what the text keeps supporting: she is afraid, she slows down, and she starts to see surrender as release. Another reader might stress the tarot, or the books she mentions, more than I do, but any reading has to explain the snake, the pause, and the leaves, and not skip them.
